Developing Google’s Smartphone AR Design Guidelines

September 9, 2026

When smartphone AR began moving from demos into products, there were few established interaction conventions. AR asked users to move through physical space, help the system understand an environment, place virtual content, and recover when tracking failed.

At Google, I helped define design guidance for these experiences as ARCore and smartphone AR were developing.

Making system understanding legible

An AR system is continuously interpreting the environment. A surface may not yet be detected, tracking can degrade, or lighting and movement can make the scene difficult to understand. Technical uncertainty therefore becomes an interaction problem: what should the user do next, and how will they know?

We developed guidance for contextual coaching, surface discovery, placement, anchoring, and recovery. Instead of relying on up-front tutorials, the interface could teach behaviors when they became relevant — showing that a surface had been found, indicating where content could be placed, or helping the user correct their movement.

Designing for physical space

AR required treating the physical environment as part of the interface. Placement distance, room size, movement, comfort, and safety all affected the experience. Users also had to learn that relevant content might exist outside the phone’s current field of view, so visual and audio cues could encourage spatial discovery.

From product patterns to platform guidance

The guidelines drew from research and analysis across shopping, education, creativity, and gaming. The challenge was to separate product-specific solutions from patterns that generalized across applications.

The resulting smartphone AR design guidelines gave teams and external developers shared principles for spatial interaction, environmental uncertainty, placement, movement, and recovery without prescribing a single visual style.
