The Best Design Tool Might Be One You Throw Away

September 4, 2026

The technology industry has always valued speed. With AI, that pressure has intensified.

Designers are expected to explore faster, prototype faster, test faster, and get to an answer faster. AI seems perfectly suited to this moment: it can generate images, write code, produce variations, summarize research, and turn an idea into something tangible in minutes.

But there is an uncomfortable tension underneath all of this.

Good design still takes time.

Understanding a problem, noticing what others have missed, exploring alternatives, testing assumptions, and exercising judgment are not necessarily improved by doing them faster. Sometimes they require the opposite: room to think.

So perhaps the most useful role for AI in design is not to accelerate every part of the process.

Moving fast shouldn’t mean thinking faster. It should mean spending less time on everything surrounding the thinking.

One surprisingly powerful way this is beginning to happen is through a new class of tools: tools that are built for a specific problem, used briefly, and then discarded.

Tools used to be products

Designers traditionally work inside a relatively stable set of tools.

Figma. Sketch. Photoshop. After Effects. Unity. Whatever the particular discipline requires.

When those tools don't quite do what we need, sometimes we build our own. A design systems team might create a Figma plugin. A prototyping team might create an internal application. A designer with technical skills might write a script to automate part of a workflow.

But historically, building even a small tool came with a certain minimum investment.

If you were making something purely for yourself, that investment often wasn't worth it. By the time you built enough of the tool for it to be reliable, you might have been better off simply doing the original task manually.

And once you had invested that much effort, there was an understandable temptation to go further.

Make it reusable. Handle edge cases. Add a proper interface. Document it. Distribute it. Support other users. Fix bugs. Maintain compatibility as Figma, operating systems, or APIs change.

A little utility starts becoming a product.

That makes sense when software is expensive to produce. If building the tool is the expensive part, you want to amortize that investment across many uses and many people.

AI is changing that equation.

Sometimes you only need the tool once

Recently, I needed to migrate a large collection of UI designs from one device size to another.

This wasn't simply a matter of scaling every frame by the same percentage. Layouts needed to change. Typography needed adjustment. Individual elements had to be resized according to a set of rules specific to the two devices.

Doing it manually would have taken days of repetitive work.

So instead, I built a Figma plugin.

I described the transformation rules, used AI to help write the code, tested it against the designs, corrected what didn't work, and got it to the point where it could handle the migration.

A task that could have consumed days became a couple of hours.

Then I stopped working on the plugin.

It didn't need beautiful onboarding. It didn't need to support arbitrary device sizes. It didn't need a settings architecture or comprehensive error handling. It didn't need to be generalized for every designer who might someday encounter a vaguely similar problem.

It had one job.

It did that job.

That was enough.

I've found myself doing this repeatedly.

For a research study evaluating typeface legibility, we needed multiple versions of the same set of interfaces rendered in roughly twenty different typefaces. The important work was comparing the typefaces: understanding what happened to legibility, hierarchy, density, and the overall experience.

Duplicating the screens, replacing fonts, finding corresponding weights, and making mechanical adjustments across every variation was necessary work. But it wasn't where the design judgment was.

So I made another tool.

Start with a reference set of screens. Give the tool the typefaces being evaluated. Select where the results should go. It generates the variations and handles much of the repetitive transformation automatically.

For another project, I needed to evaluate UI directly on a new kind of device. There wasn't an established workflow for getting static designs onto the hardware in the way I needed.

So I made a small Android app whose primary purpose was simply to get those designs onto the device so I could look at them.

None of these tools are particularly important on their own.

That is precisely what makes them interesting.

Toolmaking is becoming part of designing

The usual story about AI and designers is that designers can now code.

That's true, but it misses the more consequential change.

The important shift isn't simply that designers have acquired another production skill. It is that the economics of making software are changing enough that toolmaking itself can become a routine part of the design process.

Encounter a tedious transformation? Make a tool.

Need a strange research setup? Make a tool.

Need to visualize data in a particular way for one exploration? Make a tool.

Need to bridge two systems that were never intended to work together? Make a tool.

Then continue designing.

We normally think of a design tool as infrastructure: something relatively durable that exists before the work begins.

But tools can increasingly become ephemeral artifacts of the work itself.

You identify friction, create a tiny piece of software to remove it, use it, and move on.

In physical craft, this idea isn't unusual. A maker may build a jig specifically to hold one piece at the right angle for one operation. The jig doesn't need a business model. Nobody worries about onboarding another million woodworkers. Its value comes from helping make the thing that actually matters.

AI makes that mentality increasingly viable in software.

The value is not speed for its own sake

It would be easy to describe this simply as another productivity gain.

Something took three days. Now it takes two hours.

But I think that undersells what's happening.

In design, time is not interchangeable.

An hour spent duplicating twenty Figma frames is not equivalent to an hour spent looking at the results and realizing that your hypothesis was wrong.

An afternoon resizing UI elements is not equivalent to an afternoon exploring whether the interface should work differently in the first place.

Both occupy the calendar. Only one may require the designer's judgment.

This matters as teams are pushed to move faster.

There is a dangerous version of speed where the entire design process gets compressed. Less exploration. Less research. Fewer alternatives. Less time sitting with a difficult problem.

The result is technically faster design, but not necessarily better design.

Disposable tools offer another path.

We shouldn't use AI to make every part of design faster. We should use it to compress the parts that don't deserve our time.

That can give designers back some of the time that speed culture has taken away.

A bad product can be an excellent tool

This also requires changing some instincts about what software is supposed to be.

Many disposable tools would be terrible products.

They may contain assumptions that are specific to one project. They might fail outside a narrow range of inputs. They may have crude interfaces. They may not have automated tests, analytics, documentation, extensibility, or a sensible architecture for future development.

In conventional software development, these would all be warning signs.

For a tool intended to exist for three hours, they might be completely rational choices.

Production software has to survive users you don't know, contexts you can't predict, and changes that haven't happened yet.

A disposable tool may know exactly who its user is, exactly what data it will receive, and exactly what task it has to accomplish.

That changes the standard.

The question isn't whether this is good software.

The question is whether it gets you from the work you don't need to be doing to the work you do.

AI isn't only making it cheaper to build good software. It is making it reasonable to build software that was never meant to become a product.

Knowing what not to automate

There is an obvious trap here.

If building tools becomes nearly frictionless, designers can start automating everything.

That would be a mistake.

Some apparently repetitive work contains information.

Making several layouts by hand might expose a relationship you hadn't noticed. Adjusting typography across different contexts might develop intuition about the typeface itself. A messy manual prototype can reveal something precisely because you're close to the material.

Automation can remove labor. It can also remove observation.

So the emerging skill isn't simply knowing how to make disposable tools.

It's knowing which work should be disposable.

Which parts of this process are consuming time without contributing meaningful judgment?

Which activities should remain deliberately manual because doing them is part of understanding the problem?

Where is consistency important, and where is exploration more valuable?

The answers won't be the same for every project.

That's why this isn't primarily a coding skill.

It's a design decision.

Designing the tools around the work

For decades, designers have become proficient at adapting themselves to their tools.

We learn their interaction models. Their constraints become part of our process. We restructure work because a tool makes one thing easy and another thing difficult.

AI introduces the possibility of reversing that relationship.

Instead of constantly shaping the work around the available tools, we can increasingly shape temporary tools around the work.

Some will eventually prove broadly useful and deserve to become real products.

Most won't.

And that may be the point.

The future design toolkit may not simply contain better versions of Figma, After Effects, or whatever comes next. It may also contain hundreds of tiny pieces of software that exist briefly, solve one oddly specific problem, and disappear.

They don't need roadmaps.

They don't need communities.

They don't even need names.

They just need to give designers more time for the part of design that remains stubbornly difficult to accelerate: thinking carefully about what should be made.
