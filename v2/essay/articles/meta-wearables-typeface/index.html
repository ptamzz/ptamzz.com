Developing a Typeface for AR Displays

September 9, 2026

For Meta’s AR wearables, typography could not be evaluated independently from the display. Text that looked clear on a monitor could lose definition on a waveguide display: bright pixels produced halation, apertures closed up, and neighboring strokes could visually merge.

From display artifacts to type requirements

We evaluated text directly on hardware. A useful reference was that a white pixel could bleed roughly 13 arcminutes of light — enough to alter a glyph at interface text sizes.

That translated into specific requirements: a taller x-height for perceived size, wider side bearings to reduce merging, wider apertures to keep counters open, and angular cutouts or light-trap-like details where halation could close a shape.

The important shift was that the font outline was not the final artifact. The rendered letterform on the device was.

Developing Iris

I led the commissioning of two directions with Dalton Maag. Optimistic Iris adapted Meta’s existing Optimistic typeface for the display. Reality Sans took a more purpose-built approach, prioritizing display performance with less attachment to the existing brand form.

Testing both made the tradeoff between legibility and identity explicit. We iterated on-device and evaluated the typefaces in real interface contexts.

Iris ultimately retained the Optimistic lineage while incorporating the display-specific changes identified through testing.

Outcome

Iris became the system typeface for Meta’s AR wearable displays, including Meta Ray-Ban Display. The project established a direct path from display behavior to typography: measure the artifact, translate it into a type requirement, test it in the letterform, and evaluate it again on hardware.
